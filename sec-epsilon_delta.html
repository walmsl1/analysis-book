    Section 0.6 The \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) Property

    Continuity at a point can be described without sequences. The description is equivalent to the sequential definition; in Ross this is Theorem 17.2.
    🔗

    Theorem 0.6.1 The \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property.

    Let \(a\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\text{.}\) The function \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if and only if \(\forall\,\epsilon\gt 0\ \exists\,\delta\gt 0\) s.t. \(\forall\, x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This section uses the theorem in two ways. In A first proof: \(f(x)=3x+5\) and When the bound depends on \(x\text{:}\) \(f(x)=x^2\), it proves that a function is continuous. In Continuity as a hypothesis, continuity is a hypothesis, and the theorem tells us what that hypothesis gives us. Each part ends with Parsons problems for practice.
    🔗

    Subsection A first proof: \(f(x)=3x+5\)

    By Theorem 0.6.1, to prove that \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) it is enough to prove
    \begin{equation*} \forall\,\epsilon\gt 0\ \exists\,\delta\gt 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall\, x\in\operatorname{dom}(f) \text{ with } |x-a|\lt\delta,\ |f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon. \end{equation*}
    A proof follows the quantifiers in order:
    🔗
    • \(\forall\,\epsilon\gt 0\text{:}\) begin with “Let \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{.}\)” You do not get to pick \(\epsilon\text{;}\) it is arbitrary.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\exists\,\delta\gt 0\text{:}\) say “Choose \(\delta=\dots\text{.}\)” You pick \(\delta\text{,}\) and it may depend on \(\epsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\forall\, x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{:}\) say “Let \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{.}\)” Again, \(x\) is arbitrary.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Finally, show that \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The hard part is finding \(\delta\text{.}\) That is done in scratch work, which is not part of the proof.
    🔗
    The order of the quantifiers matters. Because \(\epsilon\) comes first, \(\delta\) is chosen after \(\epsilon\) is known, so \(\delta\) may depend on \(\epsilon\text{.}\) Problem 1 in the activity below works the same way: each row gives you \(\epsilon\text{,}\) and you find \(\delta\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 0.6.3.

    Use your three answers to Problem 1 in the activity.
    1. Find one \(\delta\gt 0\) that works for all three values of \(\epsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Is there one \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(\epsilon\gt 0\) and all \(x\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Any \(\delta\) with \(0\lt\delta\le 1/300\) works, such as \(\delta=0.003\text{.}\) A \(\delta\) that works for \(\epsilon=0.01\) also works for \(\epsilon=0.1\) and \(\epsilon=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) No. Given any \(\delta\gt 0\text{,}\) take \(\epsilon=\delta\) and \(x=a+\delta/2\text{.}\) Then \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) but \(|f(x)-f(a)|=3\delta/2\gt\epsilon\text{.}\) So \(\delta\) must depend on \(\epsilon\text{.}\) That is why the \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property puts “for all \(\epsilon\gt 0\)” before “there exists \(\delta\gt 0\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Your answers to Problem 1 suggest a rule for choosing \(\delta\) from \(\epsilon\text{.}\) Here is how to find that rule and turn it into a proof.
    🔗

    Proposition 0.6.4.

    Let \(f(x)=3x+5\text{.}\) Then \(f\) is continuous at every \(a\in\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof outline.

    First write the parts of the proof that the quantifiers dictate. Leave holes, in brackets, for the rest.
    🔗
    Let \(a\in\R\text{.}\) By the \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property, we must prove that for all \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(x\in\R\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{.}\) [Choose \(\delta\text{.}\)] Let \(x\in\R\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{.}\) We must show that \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [Use \(|x-a|\lt\delta\) to show that \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)]
    🔗
    🔗
    The outline has two holes: the choice of \(\delta\text{,}\) and the argument at the end. Scratch work fills in both.
    🔗
    🔗

    Scratch work.

    We need \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\) for every \(x\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{.}\) Simplify the left side:
    \begin{equation*} |f(x)-f(a)|=|(3x+5)-(3a+5)|=|3x-3a|=3|x-a|. \end{equation*}
    So we need \(3|x-a|\lt\epsilon\text{,}\) which is the same as \(|x-a|\lt\epsilon/3\text{.}\) If \(\delta=\epsilon/3\text{,}\) then every \(x\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\) satisfies \(|x-a|\lt\epsilon/3\text{,}\) which is what we need. This reasoning starts from the goal and works backward, so it is not a proof. The proof below runs the same steps forward.
    🔗
    This matches Problem 1: for \(\epsilon=1\text{,}\) \(0.1\) and \(0.01\text{,}\) the largest \(\delta\) that works is \(1/3\text{,}\) \(1/30\) and \(1/300\text{.}\) Any smaller positive \(\delta\text{,}\) such as \(\epsilon/4\text{,}\) works too, and a proof needs only one. Notice also that \(\delta=\epsilon/3\) does not depend on \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof. (Proposition 0.6.4)

    Let \(a\in\R\text{.}\) By the \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property, we must prove that for all \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(x\in\R\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{.}\) Choose \(\delta=\epsilon/3\text{,}\) which is positive since \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{.}\) Let \(x\in\R\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{.}\) We must show that \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(|x-a|\lt\delta=\epsilon/3\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} |f(x)-f(a)|=|(3x+5)-(3a+5)|=3|x-a|\lt 3\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{3}=\epsilon, \end{equation*}
    as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    The proof is the outline with its two holes filled in: the choice \(\delta=\epsilon/3\text{,}\) and a last paragraph that runs the scratch work forward.
    🔗

    Subsection When the bound depends on \(x\text{:}\) \(f(x)=x^2\)

    For \(f(x)=3x+5\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\) was a constant times \(|x-a|\text{,}\) so \(\delta\) came straight from solving for \(|x-a|\text{.}\) For most functions, the factor multiplying \(|x-a|\) changes with \(x\text{.}\) Take \(f(x)=x^2\) and \(a=2\text{.}\) Factoring gives
    \begin{equation*} |x^2-4|=|x-2|\cdot|x+2|. \end{equation*}
    The factor \(|x-2|\) is the one that \(\delta\) controls, but the factor \(|x+2|\) changes with \(x\text{.}\) The fix is to decide in advance that \(\delta\le 1\text{.}\) Then \(|x-2|\lt 1\) gives \(1\lt x\lt 3\text{,}\) so \(|x+2|\lt 5\) and
    \begin{equation*} |x^2-4|\le 5|x-2|. \end{equation*}
    Now \(|x-2|\lt\epsilon/5\) would finish the job. To get both \(\delta\le 1\) and \(\delta\le\epsilon/5\text{,}\) choose
    \begin{equation*} \delta=\min\{1,\epsilon/5\}. \end{equation*}
    The first problem at the end of this subsection turns this into a proof that works at every point \(a\text{;}\) at \(a=2\) its \(\delta\) is exactly this one. First, explore the graph.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 0.6.6.

    Let \(f(x)=x^2\text{,}\) \(a=2\) and \(\epsilon=1\text{.}\) Find the largest \(\delta\) such that for all \(x\) with \(|x-2|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|x^2-4|\lt 1\text{.}\) Why isn’t it the same distance on both sides of \(2\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Solve \(3\lt x^2\lt 5\) for \(x\gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The allowed \(x\) near \(2\) fill \((\sqrt3,\sqrt5)\approx(1.732,2.236)\text{.}\) The right end is closer to \(2\text{:}\) \(\sqrt5-2\approx 0.236\text{,}\) while \(2-\sqrt3\approx 0.268\text{.}\) So the largest \(\delta\) is \(\sqrt5-2\approx 0.236\text{.}\) The graph is steeper to the right of \(2\text{,}\) so the right side decides.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 0.6.7.

    Why does \(\delta=\epsilon/5\) on its own fail? Try \(\epsilon=50\text{.}\) Then explain why \(\delta=\min\{1,\epsilon/5\}\) works for every \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    With \(\epsilon=50\text{,}\) \(\delta=\epsilon/5=10\text{.}\) Then \(x=11\) satisfies \(|x-2|=9\lt 10\text{,}\) but \(x^2-4=117\ge 50\text{.}\) The bound \(|x+2|\lt 5\) needed \(|x-2|\lt 1\text{.}\) With \(\delta=\min\{1,\epsilon/5\}\text{,}\) both \(\delta\le 1\) and \(\delta\le\epsilon/5\) hold, so \(|x^2-4|\le 5|x-2|\lt 5\delta\le\epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    How \(\delta\) depends on \(a\).

    The same idea works at any \(a\in\R\text{.}\) If \(|x-a|\lt 1\text{,}\) the triangle inequality gives
    \begin{equation*} |x+a|=|(x-a)+2a|\le|x-a|+2|a|\lt 2|a|+1, \end{equation*}
    so \(\delta=\min\{1,\epsilon/(2|a|+1)\}\) works. This \(\delta\) depends on \(a\) as well as on \(\epsilon\text{,}\) while for \(3x+5\) the same \(\delta=\epsilon/3\) worked at every \(a\text{.}\) Is the dependence on \(a\) just a feature of our formula, or is it forced?
    🔗
    The next section, Section 0.7, asks exactly this question: when can one \(\delta\) serve every point \(a\) at once?
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Continuity as a hypothesis

    In Section 0.5, a convergence statement was something we knew, and each proof applied it to a positive number of its own choosing. Continuity works the same way. If \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{,}\) then the \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property (Theorem 0.6.1) holds for every positive number, so a proof may apply it to whichever positive number it needs: \(\epsilon\text{,}\) \(\epsilon/2\text{,}\) or a fixed number such as \(1\) or \(f(a)\text{.}\) The sentence has the chosen number in both places:
    🔗
    Because \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(\square\gt 0\text{,}\) by the \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property there exists \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt\square\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 0.6.8 Continuous functions keep their sign nearby.

    Suppose \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(f(a)\gt 0\text{.}\) Then there exists \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(f(x)\gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Assume \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(f(a)\gt 0\text{.}\) We must prove that there exists \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(f(x)\gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Because \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(f(a)\gt 0\text{,}\) by the \(\epsilon\)–\(\delta\) property there exists \(\delta\gt 0\) such that for all \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt f(a)\text{.}\) With this \(\delta\text{,}\) we will show that for all \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{,}\) \(f(x)\gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\) with \(|x-a|\lt\delta\text{.}\) Then \(|f(x)-f(a)|\lt f(a)\text{,}\) so \(-f(a)\lt f(x)-f(a)\text{.}\) Adding \(f(a)\) to both sides gives \(f(x)\gt 0\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 0.6.9.

    The goal has no \(\epsilon\) in it, so the proof never says “Let \(\epsilon\gt 0\text{.}\)” The number in the box is \(f(a)\text{,}\) the distance from \(f(a)\) down to \(0\text{.}\) This is the function version of the third problem in Section 0.5, where \(L-a\) went in the box. A proof with the sequential definition is possible too, but it needs a proof by contradiction.
    🔗
    🔗
