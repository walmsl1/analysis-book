    Section 0.2 Open Sets

    \(\R \text{.}\)\(\R \)open sets.

    Definition 0.2.1. Open set.

    A subset \(A \) of \(\R \) is an open set if \(\forall \, a\in A \, \exists \, \epsilon>0 \) s.t. \((a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon) \subseteq A \text{.}\) Or, spelling out the hidden quantifier in the \(\subseteq \) symbol, \(A \) is open if \(\forall \, a\in A \, \exists \, \epsilon>0 \) s.t. \(\forall \, x\in (a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon), x\in A \text{.}\)
    Roughly speaking, a set \(A \) is open we can put every element in the set inside a tiny open interval which is contained completely inside \(A \text{.}\) Let’s look at a general proof outline for showing \(A \) is open.
    \(A \) is open if... Proof outline
    \(\forall \, a\in A \) Let \(a\in A. \)
    \(\exists \, \epsilon > 0\) s.t. Define \(\epsilon. \)
    \(\forall \, x\in (a-\epsilon, a+\epsilon)\) Let \(x\in (a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon).\)
    \(x\in A \) We must show \(x\in A.\)
    For example, the interval \((c,\infty) \) is open for any \(c\in \R \text{.}\) Let’s see a proof.

    Proof.

    Let \(c\in \R. \) Let \(a\in (c,\infty).\) Define \(\epsilon = c-a.\) Let \(x\in (a-\epsilon, a+\epsilon). \) We must show \(x\in (c,\infty).\)
    Since \(x\in (a-\epsilon, a+\epsilon),\) we know \(a-\epsilon\lt x.\) And since \(a-\epsilon = c,\) we have \(c\lt x, \) which implies \(x\in (c,\infty),\) as desired.
    In the previous proof, \(\epsilon \) represented the distance from the element \(a\) to the left endpoint of \((c,\infty) \text{.}\) The interval \((c,d) \) is also open whenever \(c\lt d \text{,}\) but now there are two endpoints to consider for an element \(a\in (c,d)\text{:}\) the distance \(a-c \) to the left endpoint, and the distance \(d-a \) to the right endpoint. We will pick \(\epsilon \) to be the lesser of these two distances, which will ensure \((a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon) \) is contained completely inside of \((c,d) \text{.}\)

    Proof.

    Let \(c\lt d\) and let \(a\in (c,d) \text{.}\) Define \(\epsilon=\min(a-c,d-a)\) and let \(x\in (a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon).\) We must show \(x\in (c,d),\) meaning \(c\lt x \) and \(x\lt d.\)
    By virtue of being the minimum of \(a-c\) and \(d-a,\text{,}\) we know \(\epsilon \leq a-c.\text{.}\) Rearranging yields \(c\leq a-\epsilon, \) and since \(a-\epsilon \lt x,\) we must have \(c\lt x \text{.}\)
    Similarly, we know \(\epsilon \leq d-a \text{,}\) which yields \(a+\epsilon \lt d \) after rearranging. Hence \(x\lt a+\epsilon\leq d,\text{,}\) which implies \(x\lt d\text{.}\) Then since \(c\lt x \) and \(x\lt c, \) we know \(x\in (c,d),\) as desired.
    Observe that the only properties of \(\epsilon \) that are needed are that both \(\epsilon \leq a-c\) and \(\epsilon \leq d-a. \) There are certainly other numbers with those two properties that we could have chosen for \(\epsilon, \) such as \(\displaystyle \frac{(a-c)(d-a)}{1+(a-c)+(d-a)}.\) Using a minimum makes it clear what is important about \(\epsilon \text{.}\)
    Not every set of real numbers is open. Negating the definition of open gives us the following.

    Definition 0.2.2. Open set.

    A subset \(A \) of \(\R \) is not an open set if \(\exists \, a\in A \) s.t. \(\forall \,\epsilon\gt 0 \, \exists \, x\in (a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon)\) s.t. \(x\not\in A \text{.}\)
    For example, the interval \([c,d)\) is not open because of the inclusion of the pesky left endpoint; no open interval \((c-\epsilon,c+\epsilon) \) is contained completely within \([c,d) \text{.}\)

    Proof.

    Let \(c\lt d \) and consider \(a=c\text{.}\) Let \(\epsilon >0 \) and let \(x=a-\epsilon/2\text{.}\) Observe \(x\in (a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon).\) We must show \(x\in [c,d)\text{.}\) Since \(\epsilon >0\text{,}\) we have \(x=a-\epsilon/2\lt a=c,\) which shows \(x\lt c\) and \(x\not \in [c,d).\)
